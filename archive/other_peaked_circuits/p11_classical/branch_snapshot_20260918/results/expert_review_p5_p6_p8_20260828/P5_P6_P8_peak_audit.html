Why P5, P6 and P8 haven’t cracked

The extraction step is measuring the wrong thing, and the validation apparatus around it cannot detect the error that dominates. Both are fixable. The compute gap underneath is real but smaller than it first looked — for P8 it is an overnight run away.

Reviewed: EXPERT_REVIEW.md, MANIFEST.json, the three input QASM files, tools/run_mps_distillation.py, scripts/analyze_distillation.py, and results/expert_review_p5_p6_p8_20260828/
Candidates not supported by their evidence

The P5, P6 and P8 candidate bitstrings are consistent with containing no information at all about the hidden peaks. That is a statement about the evidence, not a claim that the strings are wrong — but nothing in the package distinguishes them from what a peak-free null model produces.

Three independent lines lead there: the confidence statistics are reproduced by a no-peak simulation; at most 1–4 % of the reported marginal signal could physically come from a peak; and the MPS fidelity, which was never recorded, is around 10−21 when you measure it — twelve orders of magnitude below the level at which a 40-qubit peak is detectable.

Ground truth

What the three circuits actually are

Worth stating up front, because the three problems are not variations on one another. P8 is a sparse planar lattice; P5 and P6 are close to all-to-all. That difference decides which method can work.

P6 has a hidden 1D backbone. The 62 heaviest edges (multiplicity ≥ 20) form a single path visiting every qubit, and carry 48 % of all CZ mass. Your runs use logical order 0…61, which is a random permutation relative to that path — so nearly every heavy gate is routed the long way. Reordering along the backbone cuts total SWAP distance by 47 % (68 510 → 36 716).

P8’s geometry is being ignored. A planar treewidth-5 graph is exactly the case a 2D ansatz exists for. An MPS flattens it; that’s a choice worth revisiting (see step 5).

Finding 1

The confidence statistics can’t see the error that matters

analyze_distillation.py reports three things: unique-strings / modal frequency, split-half Hamming distance, and bootstrap bit-sign stability. All three are resampling statistics computed on the same 1 000 shots. They estimate Monte-Carlo error.

Monte-Carlo error is not your dominant error term. Truncation bias is. Resampling the same biased samples cannot detect bias — every replicate inherits it. So the apparatus is structurally incapable of failing, whatever the MPS did.

Here is what those statistics report on synthetic data containing no peak whatsoever:

‡ Null B draws each qubit’s marginal from 0.5 + N(0, 0.12) and samples independently — a state with strongly biased single-qubit marginals and no joint peak. This is what SVD truncation produces: collapsing onto a few dominant Schmidt vectors leaves marginals far from 0.5 for reasons that have nothing to do with the hidden string.

Your three headline results all sit inside the null-model band. “62 of 62 bits above threshold” is the signature of a low-rank artifact, not of a recovered peak.

Finding 2

At most 1–4 % of the reported signal could be the peak

This one follows from your own numbers with no simulation at all. Two facts are in tension.

Fact one. All 1 000 sampled strings were unique; modal frequency was 1. If the peak carried effective weight q in the sampled distribution you would expect roughly 1000 q copies of it. Zero repeats bounds q ≲ 0.003. The peak’s own contribution to any single-qubit marginal is then at most q/2 ≈ 0.0015.

Fact two. For two independent halves of 500 shots to produce majority votes agreeing on all 62 bits, the per-bit marginals must sit at least 0.179 away from 0.5. That is over a hundred times what the peak can supply.

Finding 3

Sampling and voting is the wrong estimator by ten orders of magnitude

You want argmaxx |⟨x|ψ⟩|2. That is a joint property of the state. Per-bit marginals and majority votes over samples are the one family of estimators that throws away exactly the joint information you need.

Write q = F · ppeak for the peak’s weight in the approximate state, with F the MPS fidelity. Then:

EstimatorDetects the peak whenP8, n=40P5, n=44P6, n=62
Majority vote over 1000 shotsq ≳ 0.030.030.030.03
Beam-search argmax over the MPSq ≳ 2−n9.1×10−135.7×10−142.2×10−19
Extra slack bought by changing estimator3×10105×10111×1017

Beam search over an MPS is cheap and exact: right-canonicalise, sweep left to right keeping the best K prefixes by ‖v‖2, cost O(n K D2). There is no reason to sample.

Calibrated on a circuit where the answer is known

I built a peaked circuit by planting a string: C = Xs V′†V with V′ a noise-perturbed copy of a random V, so ⟨s|C|0⟩ = ⟨0|V′†V|0⟩ and ppeak is tunable. At n=22 the exact statevector confirms ppeak = 0.0864 on the planted string.

The useful part is the crossover. Recovery locks in at F ≈ 6×10−4, i.e. F · ppeak ≈ 200 × 2−n. That gives a concrete, answer-blind admission threshold to hold every real run to:

F ≳ 100 · 2−n / ppeak

Finding 4

Nobody measured the fidelity — and it is 10−21

Not one of the four run directories records a discarded weight, truncation error, or fidelity estimate. What they do record is that 38–41 % of compressions saturated the bond cap (P8: 184 of 484; P5 at D=128: 310 of 763), so truncation was being forced hard and silently.

I re-ran P8 tracking retained Schmidt weight through the evolution — ∏k(1 − εk), the standard fidelity proxy. It falls below the 2−40 uniform floor before the circuit is half applied.

A subtlety that matters. At D=64 the P8 beam search still returns a top candidate at 1.6×10−7 — 180 000× above the uniform floor. That looks like a peak and is not one. A heavily truncated MPS is a low-rank object and is always strongly peaked on its own. So “my candidate has high probability” is necessary but nowhere near sufficient; the fidelity gate and stability across bond dimensions are what separate a real peak from the ansatz’s own shadow. This is also, I think, why your two CircuitPermMPS seeds disagreed — each found its own artifact.

Revised · how far away is it

Closer than I first said

My first pass extrapolated from a mid-circuit measurement and concluded bond dimension of order 105–106 — effectively hopeless. The D=128 run finished afterwards and the endpoint contradicts that. Correcting it here rather than quietly.

Final retained weight across the ladder gains 2.75 decades per doubling of D on a least-squares fit — not the 0.81 decades I read off the mid-circuit slope. The gap is still twelve decades, but at that rate it closes in four to five more doublings.

Caveats, stated plainly. The per-doubling gain is noisy (1.54 to 3.88 decades) and this is a four-doubling extrapolation. Diminishing returns are likely as D approaches the true Schmidt rank. And the target itself is soft — it depends on ppeak, which nobody knows. The point is not that D=4096 will work; it is that D=4096 is cheap enough to just try, which the earlier estimate said it wasn’t.

P5 and P6 have no comparable ladder yet. P5’s slope is being measured now; P6 has not been run past D=32 with a sane ordering. Both carry ~10 SWAPs per gate against P8’s 2.3, so expect them to be materially harder.

Exact contraction remains out. A cotengra search on the P8 single-amplitude network found nothing better than contraction width 50 — worse than plain statevector, which is what you expect for a depth-129 circuit where the minimum width is bounded below by the qubit count. Roughly 1020 flops. Your original estimate of “too expensive” was correct.

Credit where due

What holds up

  • The iSWAP patch is correct. I verified the QASM gate body s·s·h·cx·cx·h numerically against the standard iSWAP matrix: identical, not a conjugate, no phase discrepancy. The explicit 4×4 matrix in the patched PEPS/PEPO runners is the right one. One worry you can drop.
  • The package’s framing is honest. EXPERT_REVIEW.md says plainly that there is no validated answer and that the candidates are not submission-grade. That is the correct call, and it is why this review can be useful rather than embarrassing.
  • Deferring the sparse tensor network and exact slicing was right. The cost estimates hold up.
  • The provenance discipline — hashes, manifests, environment capture, refusing to claim hardware results — is better than most research code. Keep it.
What to do

A protocol that can fail

In order. Steps 1–3 are cheap and change what you can claim today; 4–6 are where the real work is.

  1. Calibrate the pipeline before trusting it

    Nothing in the package has ever been shown to recover a peak it didn’t already know. make_synthetic_peaked.py plants one at any n with a tunable height and computes the exact answer up to n ≈ 26. Any method that cannot recover a planted ppeak = 0.05 at n=24 does not get run on P5.

  2. Replace sampling with beam search

    peakfind.py does this. It also prints the retained weight, the ratio to the uniform floor, the gap to the runner-up, and the Hamming spread of the top-k. Every run gets a fidelity number or it doesn’t count.

    Then run the P8 ladder to where it actually matters: D = 256, 512, 1024, 2048, 4096, one per night, stopping when the retained weight clears the gate and the candidate stops moving between rungs. This is the single run most likely to produce a real answer.

    python scripts/peakfind.py inputs/P8_grid_888_iswap.qasm \
    --bond 512 --cutoff 1e-14 --engine perm \
    --order-json results/p8_protocol_20260828/mps_orderings.json \
    --order-key P8/annealed --beam 1024 --out p8_D512.json

  3. Retire the bootstrap gate, keep the null model

    Bootstrap bit-stability and split-half Hamming should not appear in a submission argument again — ship audit_null_model.py output alongside any statistic you do quote, so a reader can see the null band. Replace the gate with two conditions: fidelity above 100·2−n/p, and the candidate unchanged across two consecutive doublings of D.

  4. Fix the site ordering, and use the permutation engine

    For P6 this is nearly free and worth 47 % of the SWAP cost. Use CircuitPermMPS rather than swap-routed CircuitMPS for P5 and P6 — at ~10 SWAPs per gate the routing, not the gates, is what destroys the fidelity.

  5. For P8, use the geometry — but not simple update

    A planar treewidth-5 lattice is the one case here where a 2D ansatz genuinely fits, and an MPS is throwing that away. Simple-update PEPS was never going to be stable on a deep circuit; it neglects the environment entirely, which is exactly what your unstable ladder shows. The right tool is boundary-MPS contraction of the 2+1D network with a controlled boundary bond χ (quimb’s TensorNetwork2D contraction machinery), or full-update PEPS. Same beam-search extraction on top, same fidelity gate. P8 is the one of the three where I would expect real progress.

  6. Multi-run consensus, with a null

    Artifacts differ between independent approximations; a real peak does not. Run several orderings and seeds, intersect the top-k lists, and compare the overlap against what independent random top-k lists would give. Under this test your two disagreeing CircuitPermMPS seeds are already evidence of failure — make that a designed check rather than an incidental observation.

Bit ordering is a real question but not today’s. It is a two-way choice between q0-first and Qiskit’s little-endian counts convention, and peakfind.py emits both. It cannot matter until there is a converged peak to order. Resolve it with a deterministic single-X reference circuit through the identical export path, once you have something to submit.
